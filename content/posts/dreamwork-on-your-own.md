Most people assume dreamwork requires someone else in the room. It doesn't. Some of the deepest work I've done with my own dreams happened alone, in bed, before anyone else in the house was awake.

Here's how to begin.

## First, what we're not doing

We're not looking anything up.

A dream dictionary will tell you that a snake means transformation. It cannot tell you what *your* snake means, in your body, on this particular morning — and that's the only meaning that will do anything for you.

So put the reference books down. What follows is a practice of listening, not decoding.

## Before bed: try some dream incubation

Dream incubation is a formal-sounding name for something simple. You ask.

Before you fall asleep, put a question to the night. Not a demand — an invitation:

- What message am I hoping for?
- What answer am I looking for right now?
- What am I trying to process?
- Where do I need guidance?

Pick one. Say it silently, or out loud, or write it at the top of tomorrow's page. Then let it go and sleep.

You won't always get a response, and almost never the tidy one you were picturing. But asking changes what you notice on waking — you come up looking for something, which is a very different way to surface than being dumped straight into the day.

And put something to write on within reach while you're at it. If you're short on time, I recommend my [Lunar Dream Map](/lunar-dream-map/) — a whole cycle fits on a single page.

## On waking: don't move yet

This is the step everything else depends on.

When you wake, stay where you are. Eyes closed if you can. No phone, no getting up, no starting the list. Let the dream come back on its own — it usually arrives in fragments.

I wrote more about why this works in [Your Dreams Are Right There](/posts/your-dreams-are-right-there/). The short version: the waking mind sorts and files, and it will file your dream straight into the bin if you let it start too soon.

## Write it down badly

Speed matters more than quality here. Spelling errors are often information, or strange word choice.  Fragments are fine. Present tense helps — *I am standing in my grandmother's kitchen and the water is rising* — because it keeps you inside the dream rather than reporting on it from outside.

Give it a title while you're at it. "The Barn Is On Fire." "Swimming With My Grandmother." A title is often the first piece of meaning to surface, and it makes the dream findable later.

## Then go to the body

This is the somatic half, and it's the part that makes this dreamwork rather than journaling.

### Start with your associations

Before you go anywhere near meaning, look at your life. What happened yesterday. What's been sitting unresolved. Who you spoke to, what you avoided, what you're dreading or hoping for.

Then widen out: where are you in your greater cycles? Where in your menstrual cycle, if you have one. Where in the season. Where in whatever larger passage you're moving through — a pregnancy, a postpartum, divorce, a grief. Dreams arrive inside a context, and [that context shapes them](/posts/we-dont-dream-like-men/).

### Name the feeling, then find it

Somatic work starts with two questions, in this order:

**What am I feeling?** Not what the dream means — what it left in you. Dread. Tenderness. Irritation. Longing. Something you don't have a word for yet is fine too.

**Where is that in my body?** Right now, as you hold the dream. Throat, chest, belly, jaw, hips, the backs of your legs.

And here's what to expect, because it's the reason most people think they can't do this:

**It is most likely a whisper.**

Not a shout. Not a wave of revelation. A slight tightening. A small warmth. A heaviness you'd walk straight past if you weren't listening for it. If you're waiting for something dramatic to announce itself, you'll miss the thing that's actually there.

### Then: explain the dream without words

Here's the question I come back to more than any other.

**How would you explain this dream without words?**

Think dream-time charades. If you had to get it across using only your body — a gesture, a posture, the shape your hands make, the way your shoulders move — what would you do?

Then actually do it. Alone in your room, no one watching. Or with a partner over morning coffee. Move it.

It feels silly, but the results are fascinating.

This is where dreams tend to give up more than they ever did on the page.

## Ask, don't answer

When something in the dream wants attention, get curious instead of conclusive. Not *what does this mean* but:

- Where do I feel this one in my body?
- What is the felt sense of it — dread, delight, grief, static?
- Where else in my life does this feeling live?
- If this feeling had an impulse, what would it be asking for?


Let the answers stay open. Dreams are never done.

## Know when to stop

Working alone has a limit, and it's worth naming plainly.

If a dream floods you — if you find yourself shaking, numb, spun out, or somewhere far from the room — that's the signal to stop and come back to the present. Feel your feet. Look around and name what you actually see. The dream will keep.

Recurring nightmares, dreams tied to trauma, and anything that leaves you dysregulated for hours are not solo material. That isn't a failure of your practice; it's the point at which a nervous system needs company. Bring those to someone trained to hold them — a therapist who works somatically, and ideally one who takes dreams seriously.

## Keep it going

A single dream is interesting. Thirty days of dreams is a map.

Patterns only show up over time —  That's why I chart mine on a wheel across a full lunar cycle rather than keeping a stack of loose pages.
