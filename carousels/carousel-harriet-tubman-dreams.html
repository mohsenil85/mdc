Caption

Harriet Tubman dreamed she was flying. Over fields, over towns, over rivers and mountains — like a bird, she said. And then a barrier she couldn't get over. A fence, or a river. On the other side, women dressed in white, reaching out their hands to pull her across. She said she recognized places on her escape routes because she had seen them first in the dreams. The visions started after a head injury. She was about thirteen. An overseer threw a two-pound weight at another enslaved person and it struck her skull instead. For the rest of her life she had sleeping spells she couldn't control — she would drop out of a conversation mid-sentence and come back describing what she had seen. Before she ran in 1849, she dreamed of horsemen riding in and children being torn away, screaming. Days later she learned she and her brothers were to be sold south. She treated the dream as the reason to go when she went. Here's the part I want to be careful about. Tubman was illiterate and left no written account of her own. Every one of these dreams reaches us through Sarah Bradford, a white writer working from interviews years later, who put Tubman's speech in heavy dialect for a sympathetic Northern audience. She called them visions, and she understood them as God talking to her directly — continuous with her waking life, not separate from it. The epilepsy explanation you'll see everywhere is a modern guess, not a diagnosis anyone made at the time. It's plausible. It isn't documented. I first encountered this in Prentis Hemphill’s What It Takes to Heal. What stays with me is the flying dream. That she saw the far side before she reached it, and there were women there already, with their hands out.
