Dream Journal

How do you cite a dream?

1
The problem

Academia will cite almost anything.

A book. A lecture. A conversation in a hallway. A footnote to a footnote.

But our dreams? nope.

2
Someone fixed this

Amy Shawanda, an Anishinaabe scholar, built a citation guide for dreams.

So that Dream Knowledge can be credited in academic writing the way any other source is.

3
Why it matters

Dreams, she writes, are how Anishinaabe people connect to the spiritual realm.

They deserve to be referenced without shyness about where the knowledge came from.

4
In her own papers

Her grandmother, who inspired the research through dreams — cited as the source she is.

Her own son, in a dream before she was pregnant, telling her he wanted to come into this world — but was waiting for her.

5
Reading her work

I realized my son deserves the same credit.

His presence was known in a dream first. I woke knowing I was pregnant, took a test, and was — to my surprise — pregnant.

6
In Shawanda's format

Stuckman-Mohseni, O. (2024).
Pregnant with Baby Number 2.
Prophecy/vision. Boulder, CO.

The dream that announced his arrival, on the page, with a year and a place.

7
The shape of it

Dreamer. Whoever the dream came to.

Year, and a title. What the dream was about.

Type. Prophecy, vision, visitation.

Place. Where you were sleeping when it found you.

Framework: Amy Shawanda.
